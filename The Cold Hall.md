# The Cold Hall

*A Rustfall Kathā*

Oct 19, 2026 · @Kristoffer Law

The coupon post at the edge of the old city was a stone pillar with an iron tongue set into it, and every crew that passed it was required to touch the tongue and say the year. Meera touched it with two fingers. The iron came away on her skin as red dust.

"Three hundred and eleven," she said.

"Three hundred and eleven," the crew repeated behind her, muffled by their hoods.

The keeper in the booth made a mark in his book. When Anu touched the tongue, he looked up, for a moment, at her hand, which did not shake, and then down again. He was paid by the Tula Daftar in the capital, and he had seen a great many crews go in.

There were five of them. Meera led, because the crew had voted her in three seasons running, and because she had passed her seven so many years ago that the law had long since stopped calling her a woman. Purushavat, the books said: like a man. A purushavat could lead a crew like any man, and on the street they still called Meera saptajit, the one who conquered seven. Tahir walked second, because he had been inside more times than anyone living, and because his lips were the faint grey-blue of a man who had survived the fever and never fully come back from it. He had passed his seventy-seven before Meera was born, and still came, because there was nothing else an ash-touched man was allowed to do. Behind him were the brothers Jai and Ravi, past their seven and still a long way short of seventy-seven. Last came Anu, who was eighteen, and new, and who had spent four years at the crucible sheds being called a boy until the crew could say it without thinking. Anu had been told the rules so many times on the road that she had started saying them in her sleep, and had learned to say them in a lower voice.

*Hood on before the gate. Glass eyes clean. Touch nothing red with your skin. If you cut yourself, say so. If you cough, say so. Moss before treasure. Out before dark.*

The old city had been called something else once. The keepers' books called it Bengaluru, and the crews called it the Plateau, and most of it was now forest growing over long low mounds that had been buildings. The tall ones had been taken down on purpose in the first years. Everyone knew that, because the stories said the old Empire had done it carefully, floor by floor, while the machines still worked. What remained were the cold halls: long concrete sheds full of black cabinets, where the old people had kept their thinking.

They reached the cold hall at midmorning. The roof had fallen in at the east end and a fig tree had come through, but the west end was still covered, and dark, and smelled of wet iron.

Tahir stopped at the threshold and raised a hand. They waited while he listened. Anu did not know what he was listening for. She asked him once, on the road, and he said, *the floor talking*, and would not explain.

"Slow," he said. "There are mats on the beams."

Meera lit the lamp. It was glass, with a ceramic base and a wick of twisted cotton, and it made a small honest light. The light went across the floor and up the cabinets, and Anu forgot to breathe.

The moss covered everything. It grew in sheets over the black cabinets, a dull bronze-green like old copper, and where the lamplight touched it, it glittered. Not everywhere. In lines. In the places where the old machines had held their gold, the moss had gathered it, grain by grain, over three hundred years, and the lines shone like writing.

"Don't stare at it," Tahir said. "Stare at the floor."

The floor was the danger. Above them, the beams that had once held the roof were furred with the grey mats that grew wherever iron rotted in the damp. Those mats were alive, and when they were disturbed they breathed out a fine dust that the hoods were made to stop. The hoods did not always stop it. Ravi had a cousin who had worked the cold halls in the north, near the old border, where the fallen fire had come down, and the mats there were black instead of grey. His cousin had coughed for a year and then stopped.

They worked the way crews always worked. Meera chose a cabinet. Jai and Ravi held the jars, glazed clay with wooden lids. Tahir scraped with a bone knife, peeling the moss away in long careful strips, and laid each strip in the jar like folding cloth. Anu's job was to hold the lamp and watch the beams. If anything above them moved, she was to say *up* and nothing else.

It was slow. A cabinet took most of an hour. The moss came away in sheets, and underneath the black metal was soft and flaking and came away too, and none of them touched it with their skin. By noon they had six jars, and the jars were heavy in a way that made Jai grin behind his glass.

"This is a good hall," he said.

"It's a good hall because it's dark," said Tahir. "Dark halls have thick mats. Watch the beams."

It was in the seventh cabinet that Meera found the glass.

It was underneath the moss, in a slot at the back, where some old hand had pushed it and never taken it out again. A square of clear glass, no wider than her palm, perfectly smooth, with a faint cloudiness inside it like breath on a cold morning. She held it up to the lamp. The cloudiness was not cloudiness. It was very small marks, rows and rows of them, so small that they made a haze. Anu leaned in to see and Meera felt her go still.

Everyone knew what it was. Everyone had heard of the glass that the old people had written in, and that the licensed scholars in the capital paid for in gold, and that the Saralpanthis broke with hammers in the market squares while the crowds watched.

"Put it back," Ravi said.

"It's worth more than the jars," said Jai.

"It's worth more than us. That's the problem. Put it back."

Meera looked at Tahir. He was the only one who had been inside long enough to have an opinion that mattered. He was quiet for a long time, and the lamp hissed, and somewhere above them a beam creaked.

"My mother's crew found one," he said finally. "Before I was born. They sold it to the Daftar."

"And?"

"And the Daftar paid them. And then the Saralpanthis heard. They burned the whole street." He shrugged. "My mother was not on the street that night. That is why I was born."

"So put it back," said Ravi.

"I didn't say that." Tahir reached out and took the glass from Meera, and turned it once in the light. "The scholars say the old people did something. At the ends of the world, where the ice is. All of them together. Everyone did it, and this is how they remembered. In here." He tapped it, gently. "If we put it back, the moss covers it and nobody ever reads it. If we take it, someone burns a street."

"Or we take it to the weigher," Jai said quietly, "and he calls it seventy-seven. You've seen it done. One glass, and the boy walks out free on his first run."

Nobody looked at Anu. Everybody was not looking at Anu.

"A seventy-seven brings questions," Tahir said. "The weigher writes it in the book. Then somebody wants to see the one it freed. Then somebody looks closely." He turned the glass in the light again. "Closely is not good for everyone."

"Up," said Anu.

They all looked. One of the grey mats on the beam above them had sagged, slowly, and as they watched a piece of it tore loose under its own weight and came down in a soft grey drift through the lamplight. It landed on the floor two paces from Ravi and broke open, and the dust came off it like smoke.

"Out," said Meera. "Jars first. Now. Don't run."

They did not run. You didn't run. Running stirred the air. They walked to the doorway with the jars held tight, breathing slow through their hoods, and Anu walked last, holding the lamp high so the others could see their feet, and when they came out into the sunlight they stood very still in a line and did not take their hoods off until Tahir said they could, a long time later, after they had stripped the outer cloth and put it on the fire and stood in the smoke.

Ravi coughed once, by the fire. Everyone looked at him. He looked back at them, frightened, and then he coughed again, and then he laughed, because it was only the smoke.

They camped at the edge of the forest that night. They would wait the three days at the coupon post before they were allowed back on the road, watching each other's lips and the whites of each other's eyes. The jars sat in a row by the fire. Six of them, heavy with moss, and when the moss was burned in the assayer's crucible it would leave a bead of gold at the bottom of each, and the weigher would write each bead into the book as jars. Some would go toward Jai and Ravi's seventy-seven. Meera had already said, without asking anyone, that the best of them would be written to Anu.

The glass was not in the jars.

Anu had seen Tahir put it somewhere when the dust came down, but she had not seen where. Back in the slot, maybe, under the moss, for the next three hundred years. Or somewhere else. She did not ask. She thought about seventy-seven, and about a weigher looking closely at a boy he had just set free, and she understood what Tahir had chosen, and for whom. She thought perhaps that was the point: that if no one asked, then no one would have to decide, and no street would burn, and the old people's writing would wait.

Near dawn she woke and saw Tahir sitting by the ashes, looking up. She followed his eyes. Low in the eastern sky, very faint, a star was moving. It slid slowly across the others in a straight line, steady as a boat, and then it faded and was gone.

"One of theirs," Tahir said quietly, when he saw she was awake. "There used to be more. My grandmother said when she was small you could see a dozen in a night."

"What are they?"

He thought about it for a while.

"Things they made," he said. "That still work. Up there, where nothing rots."

He did not say anything else, and after a while Anu went back to sleep. In the morning they touched the iron tongue at the coupon post, and said the year, and began the long walk to the capital with their jars.

# Sealed State

*A Rustfall Kathā*

Oct 20, 2026 · @Kristoffer Law

The pens froze at Vostok, so Tomas Reyes kept the log in pencil.

He liked it better anyway. A pencil line on paper did not crash, did not need a password, did not lose its battery in the cold. Every shift he wrote the time and the temperature and the state of the cryostat in a ruled notebook with a cardboard cover, in his small square hand, and every season the notebooks went into a box in the store hut, and nobody ever read them again. That was fine. Logs were not for reading. They were for the day something went wrong.

*05:02 local. Outside −64 °C. Wind light. Cryostat stable at 54 K. Cell nominal. Relay pass in 39 min.*

He blew on his fingers and looked up at the others.

---

There were six of them in the lab hut that morning, which was too many for the space and exactly the right number for the work. The hut had been built for one experiment and refitted for this one over two winters, and it was full: racks of electronics to the ceiling, the white bulk of the cryostat in the middle like a squat, frosted bell, cable trays overhead, a laser table under a dust cover, and along one wall a row of laptops where the people who had not been allowed to touch anything else sat and watched numbers.

Anaya Kerr was at the main console. She had been on the project for eleven years and lead at Vostok for three, and she was the only one of them who looked rested, which meant she had not slept at all. Grisha, the station chief, sat on an upturned crate by the door with his parka open, holding a mug of tea he had stopped drinking an hour ago. Two postdocs, Lena and Kwame, shared one chair at the laptops and took turns standing. And Hyun-woo, the cryo engineer, was lying on the floor under the cryostat with a torch in his teeth, as he had been for most of the night.

"Stop touching it," Anaya said, without looking round.

"I'm not touching it. I'm admiring it."

"Admire it from further away."

He slid out, grinning, and sat up against the rack. His beard had frost in it from his own breath. Everyone's did. The hut was heated, but heat at Vostok was a relative thing.

---

The idea, when you took away the mathematics, was simple enough to explain to a child, and Anaya had explained it to her own, once, over a bad video call from this same hut.

Inside the cryostat was a small glass cell, no bigger than a thumb, full of oxygen. Ordinary oxygen, the kind in the air, cooled until it was nearly liquid and held very still. Oxygen was a strange molecule. It had two unpaired electrons spinning the same way, which made it faintly magnetic, a little compass needle too small to see. Billions of them, in the cell, all pointing the same way along the Earth's own magnetic field.

At the other end of the world, at the new station in the Arctic, there was another cell, the same as this one, held the same way.

And this morning, if everything worked, they were going to lean all the tiny compasses in this cell a little, by an amount only Vostok knew, and send that lean to the other one. The alignment itself was the same at both ends; that was only the baseline, the shared idea of which way was up. The lean was the message. Not the oxygen itself. Just the state. The information. Through the satellite overhead, through the planet's own magnetic field, which ran from here to there through the core like a single enormous thread.

"Like sending a shape," her daughter had said, at seven, frowning.

"Exactly like sending a shape."

"Why?"

Anaya had not had a good answer for a seven-year-old. The answer was that nobody had ever done it with anything so large or so far, and that it would tell them something about how quantum information behaved in curved spacetime, along a gravitational field, across an entire planet, that nobody had ever been able to test. The answer was that it was beautiful. The answer was that it would never have been possible for any one country, and so for once it had not been tried by any one country. The cell in front of her had been built in one place and the laser in another, the satellite had been launched by a third, the funding had come from a dozen governments that could not agree about anything else, and the people in this hut had come from four continents to sit in the coldest place on Earth and freeze their beards.

"Because we can," she had said, in the end. "And because we're doing it together."

---

*05:14. Cryostat stable. North reports ready. Relay pass in 27 min.*

The Arctic station came through on the voice line, a woman's voice with a crackle on it, loud in the small hut. "Vostok, North. We're cold and we're ready. How's your bell?"

"Ringing," Anaya said. "Stable at fifty-four. You?"

"Same. Same numbers. It's a little eerie, honestly." A laugh. "Two cells, two ends of the Earth, doing exactly the same thing."

"That's the point."

"I know it's the point. It's still eerie."

Lena leaned over and whispered to Kwame, and Kwame laughed, and Anaya let it go. They were all a little giddy. Two winters of building. Eleven years of papers and proposals, committees, grant reviews, one near-cancellation, a year when the whole collaboration had very nearly broken apart over things that had nothing to do with physics at all. And here they were, with twenty-seven minutes on the clock.

"Is it chilling?" Kwame asked, to no one in particular.

"Warming," said Grisha. Three bottles of champagne had come down on the last flight in, packed in a cooler, and Grisha had spent the season explaining to visitors that at Vostok the cooler was there to stop them freezing solid. They were sitting in a box under the heater in the mess hut now, and twice in the night someone had gone to check on them, as if they might have wandered off.

"You'll get your champagne," Anaya said. "Doctor Mensah."

Kwame went pink above his frosted beard. His thesis had been approved by email four days ago, the message arriving in the middle of a whiteout, and he had read it out loud to the whole station at dinner. Lena's had been approved the week before. They had both written their final chapters about this experiment without knowing how it would end, which their committees had found charming and slightly alarming in equal measure.

"Doctor and Doctor," said Hyun-woo from the floor. "And me, still just a man who fixes the fridge."

"A very cold fridge."

"The coldest fridge in the coldest place on Earth. Put it on my headstone."

Lena said, "If this works, who gets the Nobel?"

There was a groan round the hut, because they had all had this conversation before, usually around two in the morning.

"Three people maximum," said Kwame. "There are four hundred names on the author list."

"They'll give it to the satellite," said Hyun-woo.

"They'll give it to the committee that nearly cancelled us," said Lena, "for their courage in changing their minds."

"They'll give it to Anaya," Grisha said, from his crate, and nobody laughed at that one, and Anaya didn't look round, but Tomas saw the back of her neck colour a little under her collar.

"They'll give it to whoever gets to Stockholm first," she said. "Watch the numbers."

---

The experiment was not the only thing running at Vostok that morning. It never was.

Through the wall, in the next hut, the magnetometers for the geomagnetic survey were logging, as they had every minute for decades. Two huts down, the ice-core people were asleep, their drill parked for the season three kilometres above the dark water of the buried lake. Out on the snow, a mast of instruments for a cryogenic detector that Hyun-woo called "the other experiment" and nobody else ever mentioned was humming under its own insulated cover. The station was a small town of instruments in the middle of nothing, and every one of them was listening to something.

Grisha said, from his crate, "Every time you people switch something on, my generator coughs."

"It won't cough this time."

"You said that last time."

"Last time it was the coffee maker."

"You said that too."

---

*05:29. Laser locked. Entangled pairs nominal. Bell-state analyser armed. Relay pass in 12 min.*

*Run 7. Sealed state loaded: tilt 14.4°, azimuth 77°. NOT FOR VOICE.*

The sealed state was the point of the whole morning. North could not know it; that was what made it a test. Anaya had chosen the numbers herself, months ago, and told only Tomas, because someone had to write them down. Fourteen point four for her daughter's birthday, the fourteenth of April. Seventy-seven for the institutions in the collaboration, which had been seventy-six until a lab in Nairobi joined in the spring and somebody baked a cake. And this was run seven, because six passes this season had been scrubbed: for weather, for faults, and once, unforgivably, for the coffee maker. When North read back what its own cell had turned into, the two sets of numbers would go side by side on this page, and either they would match or they would not.

Tomas wrote it slowly. His hand was cold, and he wanted the letters right. Someday, he thought, somebody would open this notebook and read this page, because this was the page that mattered, and he wanted them to be able to read it.

He looked round the hut. He found he wanted to remember it: the frost on the window, the frost in Hyun-woo's beard, the glow of the screens on Lena's face, Grisha's cold tea, Anaya's straight back at the console. The racks of equipment with their thousands of tiny contacts, gold and copper and tin. The cryostat, frosted white. The thumb of oxygen inside it, which they had never seen and never would, holding still at fifty-four kelvin with all its tiny compasses pointing north.

He was, he realised, very happy.

---

At 05:38 the relay satellite rose over the horizon, thirteen hundred kilometres up, invisible, crossing the pale sky of the Antarctic spring.

"Acquisition," Kwame said. "Signal good. Link to North good."

"North, Vostok. We have the relay."

"So do we. Your cell's lovely from here, Vostok. All the numbers lining up."

"Hold the line."

There was a sequence. There was always a sequence: checks and cross-checks, two people reading each number aloud, the voice line from the north calling back each one. Lena and Kwame stood shoulder to shoulder at the laptops, reading. Hyun-woo was on his feet now, one hand on the cryostat's frame without quite touching it, the way you rest a hand near a sleeping animal. Grisha had put down his tea.

"Ninety seconds," Anaya said.

The window was short. The satellite would be overhead for a few minutes, in line of sight to both stations at once, the thread through the Earth's core and the path through the sky lining up for the brief time the whole design depended on. If they missed it, the next one was in a hundred and twelve minutes. Nobody wanted to wait a hundred and twelve minutes.

"Sixty."

Tomas picked up the pencil.

*05:40. All stations go. North go. Relay go.*

He left a space after it for the next line, the one he would write in a minute, when it was done. He did not know what it would say. Success, or failure, or something else, something nobody had a word for yet. He held the pencil over the empty space and waited.

"Thirty."

The voice from the north, crackling: "From all of us up here, Vostok. Whatever happens. It's been a privilege."

Anaya smiled at the console. "From all of us down here."

"Ten."

The hut was perfectly quiet. The generator hummed somewhere beyond the wall, and did not cough. The racks breathed their warm air. Outside, the wind had dropped completely, and the snow lay flat and blue to the edge of the world.

Anaya put her hand on the switch. It was not really a switch. It was a key on the keyboard, a command already typed and waiting, and the switch was only the moment when someone decided to send it. But they had all agreed, years ago, half joking, that when the moment came they would call it the switch, because a thing this large deserved a switch.

"Together, then," she said.

"Together," said the voice from the other end of the world.

She pressed it.

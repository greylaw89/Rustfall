# The Machine-Woman and the Metal

*A Rustfall Kathā*

Oct 19, 2026 · @Kristoffer Law

The machine woke at dusk with a sound like rain starting, and for the first time in her life Asha was the only one there to hear it.

She stood at the far end of the reading room with her hands pressed flat against the apron over her sari and listened, the way Grandmother Uma had taught her. First the water: the sluice opening in the channel outside, the paddles of the wheel taking the weight. Then the long wooden shaft that ran through the wall, groaning as it began to turn. Then the hum, low and rising, from the great clay wheel spinning inside its ring of dull pale wire.

*Nothing metal may move.* It was the first rule, and the first thing Grandmother had made her say, three years ago, on the day the Daftar sent her up from the copy-hall. Asha had asked why. Grandmother had looked at her for a long moment and said, *Because the ones who built it said so, and the ones who forgot the rule are not here.* That was all. The shaft was wood. The wheel was fired clay set with black stones that pulled at one another. Only the pale wire stood still in its ring, never touched, never turned.

The rule held for the people in the room as much as for the machine. On Asha's first morning Grandmother had slid the bangles from her wrists, the thin glass ones and the single bright one her mother had saved for, and put them in a cloth bag, and tied the bag shut. Readers wore nothing that rang or shone. Asha's sari was plain cotton the colour of unwashed rice, wound and pleated and knotted at the waist with no pin to hold it, and her blouse closed with cloth ties. Her hair was bound with cotton cord and a comb of neem wood. Around her right wrist she wore the reader's cord, dark blue thread with three knots in it, one for each year she had served. Grandmother's cord had so many knots it had gone hard as a root.

Then the bottles.

There were hundreds of them, standing in rows on long tables of polished stone, each one a small clear bottle with nothing inside it at all. Nothing: not water, not air, not even the breath of the room. Asha did not know how the old masters had put nothing into a bottle, or how they had sealed it in. Nobody she had met knew. As the hum rose, a thread of fire appeared in the heart of each one, faint at first and then steady, until the whole room was lit with a low warm glow like a field of embers.

"Mother is awake," said Kiran from the doorway.

He was eleven, the son of the woman who cooked for the House of Readers, and he was not supposed to be above the kitchen stairs. He wore a kitchen boy's short wrap knotted at the hip and a cloth over one shoulder that smelled of woodsmoke and mustard seed. In three years he would stand before the examiners like every child in the Empire, and a cook's son who failed would be ash-touched before supper, and in undyed grey by morning. Grandmother had stopped telling him so. Asha did not know if she was allowed to stop telling him so too, so she said nothing, and he came in.

"Where's the old one?"

"Coughing," Asha said. "She said I'm to do it tonight." She heard her own voice, and it sounded smaller than she meant it to. "Bring the lamp. And the stick."

The stick was a length of bamboo with a soft cloth wrapped around its tip. The lamp was the hall's own: a shallow dish of fired clay with a lip pinched up to hold the wick, a twist of cotton lying in sesame oil, the whole of it set inside a chimney of clear glass so the flame would not gutter when it moved. Kiran carried the lamp and Asha walked the rows, and every so often she leaned in and touched the stick to the base of a bottle, where two thin wires came out through the glass and met the sun-metal. She did not know what the touching did. Grandmother did it every night, so Asha did it.

Everything that touched the air was sun-metal. That was the second rule. Inside the bottles, where there was nothing, the old masters could use any metal they liked. But where the wires came out of the glass they were made of a grey, stubborn metal that no one could melt, and where they met the rest of the machine they met sun-metal, the bright yellow kind that never dulled, never darkened, never let go of its shine. It ran along the tables in flat ribbons, hammered thin, from bottle to bottle and row to row. Where two ribbons had to be kept apart there were lines of black pencil-stone drawn on fired clay. Where the machine needed to hold its breath, there were little stacks of clear flake-stone and pale leaf, light as ash, pressed together like the pages of a closed book.

"This one is cold," Kiran said.

He was right. In the fourth row, one bottle had no thread of fire in it. Asha held the lamp close. The thread was whole. The glass was whole. But at the base, where a bright ribbon should have shone, there was a dark crust, grey-black, like a scab.

She knew this one. Grandmother had shown her twice.

"Moon-metal," she said.

"What's that?"

"The white kind. The one that goes black." She took out her knife, a sliver of ceramic in a wooden handle, and began to scrape at the crust. "The old ones ran short of sun-metal, Grandmother says. So in some places they used moon-metal instead. It used to last. It doesn't anymore."

"Why not?"

Asha did not answer, because she did not know. Grandmother said the rust was hungrier now than in her own grandmother's day. The coupon-keepers at the city gate said so too, though they said it in numbers Asha could not follow. Some people in the market said it would get hungrier forever. Some said the Empire held it on a chain, against an ancient enemy over the tallest mountains to the north, where the people with blue lips sometimes came from, and the food that fed you well while slowly making you sick. Asha had seen the chain, on the man at the Daftar. She had never seen it hold anything.

The repair took most of the first hour. She cut the dead crust away, and from her pouch she took a small curl of sun-metal wire, and she bent it the way she had watched Grandmother bend it, and pressed it into place with a bone burnisher until it caught the light. Her hands were shaking. The first time, she pressed too hard and the curl slipped, and she had to find it on the floor by lamplight, on her knees, while Kiran held his breath.

The wire had come up from the Tula Daftar that morning with a slip of paper tied to it. Asha had picked the slip apart letter by letter in the Empire's script, the only script she could read with any confidence. *Bangle,* it said, and a number, and a street in the lower city, and a word she did not know. Somebody's wedding bangle, probably. Somebody's grandmother's. She had folded the slip and put it in her sleeve. Kiran could not read it. Kiran could not read anything. Neither could his mother, or anyone in the kitchens, or anyone on the street the bangle had come from.

When the bottle glowed again, Asha let out a breath she had been holding since dusk.

Then she went to the reading bench, because that was the part she was most afraid of.

The reading bench was the heart of the machine. On it, under a hood of black cloth, sat a square of glass no wider than her palm. It was old: older than the House, older than the Empire, carried south by a crew whose names were written in the House's books in letters Asha could not read. Over the glass hung a column of lenses and two clear crystals that split one picture into two. Beneath it burned a smaller lamp of the same clay and oil, its flame shut inside a glass chimney and its light gathered upward by a curved mirror of polished black stone. Above it sat a thing Grandmother called the eye: a grey stone disc that whispered when light fell on it.

The machine looked at the glass through the eye. That was what Grandmother said, and Asha believed it, though she did not understand it. There were marks inside the glass, too small to see, and the machine found them one by one in the dark, and thought about them, and then, very slowly, it punched holes into a strip of palm-leaf paper that crept out of a slot at the far end of the room.

On a good night, Grandmother said, it read a few dozen of the old letters.

Asha could read eleven of the old letters. She had been learning for three years.

She sat by the slot through the dark hours with Kiran asleep against her shoulder, and she fed the strip through her fingers as it came, the way Grandmother did. At the darkest hour, when the water was lowest and the hum had softened, she took the strip to the lamp and tried.

Most of it was nothing to her. Rows of holes, and under the holes the shapes they made, shapes she had seen on the plates in Grandmother's room but could not name. She found one she knew. Then another. She copied them onto a scrap of clay tile with a stick of pencil-stone, slowly, so she would not get them wrong.

There was one word she could read whole. Grandmother had taught it to her in her first year, because, she said, it came up often in the glass, and a reader should know it when she saw it.

*Together.*

And near it, a mark Grandmother had shown her once on a plate, and touched with a fingertip, and then covered with her hand. A mark that meant one of the two ends of the world. Asha did not know which end. She did not know what was there, except ice, and the stories of crews who went north and did not come back.

She sat very still with the strip in her hands.

"What does it say?" Kiran asked. He was awake after all.

"I don't know," Asha said, and it was true.

She would carry the strip to Grandmother's bed in the morning. Grandmother would read it, or try to, through her cough. And then one of them would carry it to the Daftar, where the man in the Daftar's starched white, with the heavy chain of his office across his chest, would weigh it, and keep it, and decide what it had said. *A reader does not read,* he had told Asha on the day she was licensed. *A reader reports. The Empire decides what has been read.* Painted on the market walls, for the people who could not read anything, the Empire's answer was already written in pictures: a wheel of rust, held in the Empire's hand.

Kiran was looking at the clay tile.

"Which one is that?" he said, pointing.

Asha hesitated. Then she wiped the old marks away with her thumb, and on the clean clay she drew, in the Empire's letters, the only ones she was sure of, five shapes, one after another.

"That's your name."

He stared at it. He touched it, very lightly, the way the crews at the city gate, in their leather hoods and charcoal-blackened mouth-cloths, touched the red, crumbling tongue on the coupon post.

"Is it allowed?" he said. "For me?"

"No."

"Then why?"

Asha looked down the long room at the rows of small orange lights, at the bright ribbons that had been somebody's wedding and were now a road for something old and dead to speak along, in a language almost no one living could hear. She thought about the word on the strip. She did not know what it meant, here. She did not know what anything in this room really meant. But she knew the shape of it.

"Because in three years," she said, "three people will sit across a table from you and decide what you are. A weigher, a man from the Daftar, and an old master who might be looking for someone. The first two want you to fail." She pushed the tile across to him. "The third one only needs a reason to argue. Give him one."

Outside, the water ran on in the channel, and the wooden shaft turned, and the clay wheel spun inside its ring of still pale wire. Nothing metal moved. The machine went on reading in the dark, one mark at a time, to no one who could understand it.

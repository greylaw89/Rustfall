# What the Sea Keeps

*A Rustfall Kathā*

Oct 20, 2026 · @Kristoffer Law

The first thing Lakshmi Raman saw of the end of the world was an aeroplane, burning, coming down slow over the Bay of Bengal with the sunrise behind it.

She was on the roof of the Kalpa building in Siruseri, south of Chennai, with a coffee, because the night shift had run long and the roof was the only place on the campus where you could see the sea. It was 06:11. Ten minutes later she saw a second plane. By seven her phone had stopped holding a call for more than a few seconds at a time, and the smell had reached her, the smell everyone in the world would remember afterwards: hot metal, struck matches, electrical burning, as if every wire on earth had been run a little too hot.

Kalpa Archives wrote data into glass. That was the whole business. A femtosecond laser, firing pulses a few quadrillionths of a second long, drew tiny structures inside squares of fused silica, layer under layer, and each structure held a few bits in the way it bent polarised light. A finished plate was about the size of a coaster. It held a library. Nothing short of a furnace could hurt it. Kalpa sold that to banks and insurers and film studios who wanted their records to outlast fires and floods and their own companies, and for three years the business had gone well, and nobody, including Lakshmi, had ever really expected the glass to be needed.

Most of the plates that actually paid the bills were vanity. When a creator on the biggest video site passed a million subscribers, the site's partner team ordered a Kalpa plate holding every video he had ever posted, set in a walnut frame with his name engraved on a brass plaque, and shipped it to him as a trophy. The creators loved them. They made videos unboxing them, and those videos ended up on other creators' plates. The free online encyclopedia had paid Kalpa to write its whole text, every language, as a demonstration: one plate flown to orbit and back as a space test of the glass, one left on the Moon aboard a private lander, and a few hundred more given away as thank-you gifts to donors who gave enough. There were football clubs with every match they had ever played, a band with its whole catalogue, a studio with its films, a few rich families with their wedding albums. In three years Kalpa had written a little over four thousand plates. Almost all of them had left the building: to offices and museums and glass cabinets in private homes, on every continent, most of them in cities nobody at Kalpa would ever see.

She went downstairs to the writing hall, where the night crew were standing around the big writer looking at the floor-mounted displays flicker. Arun Pillai, who ran the laser, looked up at her and said, "Something is eating the electronics," and she knew from his face that he meant it literally.

---

It took them a day and a half to understand what it was, and they understood it, in the end, because of a glovebox.

The materials lab on the second floor kept one, a sealed steel-and-glass cabinet filled with argon, worked through thick rubber gloves, for handling the coating chemistry on the plates. On the second morning a postdoc named Fathima noticed that everything on the bench outside it had gone dull and freckled overnight, the copper connectors green, the steel tools brown at the edges, and that everything inside it was exactly as bright as it had been the day before.

"It's the oxygen," she said, standing in Lakshmi's office doorway with her hands still red from the gloves. "I don't know why. But anything that can't touch oxygen is fine."

Her son got through first. Karthik was nineteen and in his second year at the institute in Guindy, up the old road to the north, and he was calling from his hostel, where the lights had gone and somebody's motorbike had caught fire in the parking lot. He was fine, he said. Everyone was fine. Did she know what was happening? She didn't. Then, because he was nineteen and frightened and trying not to sound it, he started complaining that he had lost his biology book, the big one on plants. He'd left it in the department reading room, on the shelf by the window, and now the doors were jammed and he couldn't get back in, and he had a test on Friday. She laughed, and told him there would be no test on Friday, and the line dropped.

Lakshmi rang everyone she could still reach. Most of the numbers were dead. Anand Krishnan, an old friend from her doctoral years, now a physicist at IISc in Bengaluru, picked up on the fourth try. The line was full of hiss. He had been on the phone for thirty hours, he said, to anyone left: Singapore, Nairobi, a lab in Quito. The northern labs had gone quiet one by one, Europe first, then Moscow, then the American coasts, then Japan. Before they went, he said, they had all been seeing the same thing, and it was worse the further north they were. Much worse.

"What is it?"

"Nobody knows." She could hear him trying to keep his voice level. "People are throwing words around. Vacuum decay, a phase change in the vacuum. Loop quantum gravity. Something that couples to oxygen and to gravity at once. Lakshmi, the rates map onto latitude, onto gravity. And there was a joint experiment running at the poles, a big one, everyone was in on it, Vostok and a new station in the Arctic, there were press releases last year. I'm not saying it's that. I'm saying everyone is saying it."

"How long do we have?"

"Here? Weeks. Up north it's already over."

---

They had the numbers by the end of the second day, worked out on paper because the computers were not to be trusted. The electronics at Siruseri were failing on a curve. Fathima plotted it on squared paper with a pencil, from the failures they had logged, and drew the line forward. Seventeen days, maybe eighteen. The grid would go before that. The writer itself, in open air, sooner still.

"So we put the writer where there is no air," Arun said.

It took them three days. The building had a bulk liquid-nitrogen tank behind the loading dock, ten thousand litres, kept for the cooling plant. They built a tent around the writer out of heavy polythene sheet and timber battens, sealed the seams with tape and wax, ran a hose from the tank through an evaporator coil, and kept the tent at a few pascals above outside pressure so that whatever leaked would leak out, not in. Inside the tent the writer and its control computer ran in clean nitrogen, and stopped dying. Outside, everything else went on rotting: the chiller pumps, the air handlers, the grid. Arun spent his days in a mask, nursing the chillers, swapping in spare pumps from the store as each one seized. The pumps were moving metal, and moving metal went fastest.

The grid failed on the eighth day. The building had two diesel generators. The new one had an electronic governor and was dead within the hour. The old one was a Kirloskar from the nineties, purely mechanical, that nobody had wanted to throw away, and it ran.

---

The argument about what to write began on the third day and never really ended.

The building's customers had contracts. Kalpa had been paid to write bank ledgers, insurance records, a studio's whole film library, satellite imagery for a mapping firm. Their racks of hard drives were already starting to fail, and Lakshmi had people sealing the best of them in bags with oxygen absorbers, little sachets of iron powder from a food-packaging firm down the road, which soaked up the oxygen in a bag in an afternoon and worked faster now than they ever had. The irony was not lost on anyone. Iron, eating the thing that was eating iron.

But the writer could only write so fast. A plate took most of a day. In seventeen days, if nothing broke, they could write perhaps forty.

On the fourth night she called the staff together in the writing hall, by candlelight, because the lights were on the generator and the generator was for the writer.

"We are not writing the bank ledgers," she said. "There will be no banks."

Nobody argued.

They wrote medicine. Surgery, anaesthesia, sterile practice, the whole of a standard pharmacology. How to grow penicillin in glass and clay, and how to find out if it had worked. They wrote chemistry, and mathematics, and how to make glass, and how to make lenses from glass, and how to make a vacuum in glass and seal it, because Arun said vacuum would matter, though he couldn't yet say why. They wrote agriculture without machines. They wrote how to smelt iron in a clay furnace, and Fathima laughed and laughed when she typed the heading, and then she cried, and then she went on typing.

---

The second argument was worse.

"We could write forty different plates," Arun said. "Forty different libraries. Or twenty, twice."

"One copy of everything," said one of the engineers, "or two copies of half."

They went round it for hours. A single copy of everything, in one building, in one city, at the end of the world. Or half the knowledge, but enough that losing one plate wouldn't lose it. In the end it was Fathima who said what settled it.

"Nobody can read these," she said. "Not for a long time. They need a machine to decode them, and there won't be any machines. Whoever finds these is going to find them by accident, years from now, maybe hundreds of years. One copy in a basement in Siruseri is a library nobody finds. Two copies is a chance. Ten is a better chance."

"We can't write ten of everything."

"No. So we write two of the important things, and we send them as far apart as we can. And we write the first layer so that someone with a magnifying glass can start."

That became her job. Every plate got an outer layer written large enough to read with a good microscope: no code, no machine, just pictures and numbers. Counting, in dots. A diagram of a lens. A drawing of light bending through a crystal, and the crystal turning, and the dots inside appearing. A hand pointing inward: *there is more in here; this is how you see it.* She drew them at night by lamplight, in pencil first, then cleaned them up on the one laptop she kept in a nitrogen bag and took out only to save files.

---

The idea of the ships came from an oceanographer.

His name was Joseph D'Souza and he worked at the ocean institute up the coast, and he arrived on the tenth day on a bicycle, sweating, because his car had died and the institute's boats were all at the pier with their engines seized. He had heard about Kalpa from a cousin. He had come because he had a map.

He spread it on the writing-hall floor. It was a chart of the Bay of Bengal, with arrows drawn on it in pencil.

"In October the current along this coast turns," he said. "It runs south. Anything adrift off Chennai goes south, down past Sri Lanka, and then the winter current carries it west, across the top of the Indian Ocean. To the Maldives. Socotra. Somalia. Some of it, if it floats long enough, to East Africa." He looked up at her. "You want the glass far apart. The sea will take it far apart for you."

"Ships don't float forever."

"Steel ships float for years," he said. "Even now. The engines go first, the electronics, the pumps. A hull is thick. It rots slowly. A ship with no engine is just a raft that drifts."

They went to the port the next morning.

---

The port was full of ships that were not going anywhere. Their bridges were dead, their engines' control systems gone. Some of the crews had already walked off. A few masters were still aboard, waiting for orders that weren't coming.

And a few ships were still working. Old ships, mostly: coastal freighters with engines built before engines had computers, fishing trawlers with simple diesels, a sailing training vessel belonging to the navy that had wooden masts and a canvas suit of sails. Lakshmi went from ship to ship with Joseph and his chart and a box of glass, and asked.

She did not ask them to deliver the plates anywhere. There was nowhere to deliver them. She asked them to carry them away. To go where they were going anyway, Colombo or Jaffna or Port Louis or anywhere, and if their engines failed, to drift, and if their ship was going down, to put the box in the water, because the glass would sink and the glass would last.

The master of an old coaster called the *Kaveri Star*, a heavy, grey-haired man who had been at sea for thirty years, turned a plate over in his fingers in the sunlight.

"What's in it?"

"Medicine. How to make glass. How to grow food without fertiliser. How to make a lens."

He looked out at the harbour, at the dead ships with their streaks of rust already bleeding down their sides after only twelve days.

"We are going to Colombo," he said. "After that, I don't know." He put the plate back in the box. "Give me two boxes."

The crates were Arun's idea. Nails would rust through in months, so the carpenters at the institute built them with wooden pegs, the way boatbuilders on that coast had built for a thousand years, and packed the plates in coir from the coconut-fibre works, which floated and did not rot quickly and cost nothing. On each lid Fathima painted the same picture she had etched on every plate: an eye, a lens, and a hand pointing inward.

Twenty-three vessels in all took crates. The *Kaveri Star*. The navy's sail-training ship, bound for Kochi and then, its captain said, wherever the wind would take a ship that had no need of engines. Seven trawlers. A cargo ship with a dead engine and a tug that still worked, which towed it out beyond the harbour and let it go, so that it would drift south with the current like a log. A tanker that nobody could sail, cut loose on purpose. Fishing boats from the villages down the coast, wooden-hulled, whose owners took a box each and promised nothing.

Joseph stood on the breakwater and watched the cut-loose cargo ship turn slowly in the current and start to move away down the coast without a soul on board.

"That one," he said, "will be the last to land. If it lands. Years, maybe."

---

The writer failed on the eighteenth day.

It was the chiller. The last spare pump seized a little after midnight, and Arun took the laser down before it overheated, and sat in the tent's doorway with his mask pushed up on his forehead and his face in his hands. They had written thirty-one plates. Twenty-eight of them were at sea.

There was one plate in the writer, half finished. Lakshmi stood looking at it through the polythene for a long time.

She had been keeping notes. Everyone had. Fathima's sketches, Joseph's chart, the failure curves, Anand's last phone call before the Bengaluru lines went: everything they knew and didn't know about what had happened. She had been saving a plate for them, meaning to write it last, and now there was no time to write all of it.

They had about an hour of the old diesel's fuel left, and a few hours of life in the laser if they ran it without cooling and let it cook itself. Arun said he'd try.

There was room on the half-written plate for perhaps a few pages.

She wrote them by hand on paper first, in English, because English was the language the glass was most likely to meet someone in, she thought, and she was wrong about that, but she could not have known. Then she typed it into the laptop in its bag, and Arun fed it to the writer, and they all stood around the tent and watched the beam they could not see draw the words they could not see into the glass.

*We don't know what happened. On the morning of the first day the metal began to fail, everywhere at once, and worst in the north. We think it has to do with oxygen, and with gravity. Some say the vacuum itself has changed. Some say it was the work being done at the poles, by many nations together. We do not know if that is true. We are writing this down because no one else may be able to.*

*Here is what we were able to save. It is not everything. It is not enough. We chose medicine first, and how to make glass and lenses, and how to grow food. We made two copies of what we thought mattered most, and sent them to sea. Forgive us for what we did not write.*

And at the end, because Fathima asked her to, the names of everyone who had stayed.

The laser died twenty minutes after the last line. The plate was finished.

---

Lakshmi walked down to the shore at dawn. The sea was flat and grey-green and empty all the way to the horizon. Somewhere out there, south of her, twenty-three ships were drifting or sailing or sinking, and inside them, packed in coconut fibre, were thirty clear squares of glass that held the best of what the world had known, in a code that nobody would be able to read for a very long time.

She had kept the last plate. It was in her pocket. She took it out and held it up against the light, and for a moment the low sun came through it and threw a faint pattern of colours on her hand: the outer layer, Fathima's drawings, too small to see, bending the light anyway.

Then she put it back in her pocket and walked home, because she had a family in Velachery and had not seen them in nine days, and there was nothing left in the building that needed her.

---

*Not all the glass the world finds came from the ships. Kalpa's older plates turn up too, thousands of them, in the ruins of offices and the cabinets of houses on every continent, wherever someone once set a trophy on a shelf. Most of them hold nothing anyone needs. When the reading machines in the south at last learned to work through a whole plate, the first they finished held only the titles of eleven years of videos made by a man cooking in a kitchen in some lost northern city, and beside each title a number: how many people had watched. The readers copied the numbers out in full. They had never imagined so many people.*

*Most of the ships never arrived anywhere. The sea kept them. It still gives them back, now and then: a clear square of glass in the wrack line on a beach in the Maldives or on the long sands below the Horn of Africa, worn a little at the edges, catching the light. Under a good lens there are pictures in it, an eye, a lens, a hand pointing inward. And deeper down, where only the reading machines can go, words in a language almost no one living speaks. One of them comes up so often that every reader learns it first.*

*Together.*

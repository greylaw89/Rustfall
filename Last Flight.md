# Last Flight

*A Rustfall Kathā*

Oct 20, 2026 · @Kristoffer Law

The sun was going down behind them, somewhere over the Colorado line, and the whole of Nebraska lay ahead in a long blue dusk. Maya Okafor had the airplane. She liked this leg for exactly this: thirty-five thousand feet, the autopilot holding everything steady, the farm towns coming on one by one beneath them like someone lighting candles along a table.

Ray Delgado was eating almonds out of a paper cup and complaining about his daughter's car.

"Transmission," he said. "Four years old."

"They don't build them like they used to."

"They never built them like they used to. That's the joke."

At 7:41 by the panel clock, nothing happened. Later she would try to remember that minute, the way people try to remember the last ordinary thing, and there was nothing in it. No bump, no sound. The almonds. The dusk. The engines turning the same note they had turned since Denver.

At 7:44 she smelled it.

Hot metal, and under it something sharp, like a struck match held too close. Ray put the cup down.

"You got that?"

"I've got it."

They ran it the way they had been trained to run it, because training is what you have when you don't have anything else. Smoke, fumes, odor. Masks on. The oxygen came cold and rubbery into her face and the smell did not go away, which meant it was not in the air system. It was in the cockpit. It was in everything.

The captain's display flickered, came back, flickered again. On her side the engine page jumped and settled. Exhaust gas temperature on the left engine was climbing in a smooth, unhurried line, as if somebody were turning it up with a dial.

"Left EGT," she said.

"I see it." Ray pulled the left thrust lever back an inch. The number kept climbing. He pulled it to idle. The number kept climbing.

---

Denver Center was a single long scream.

That was how she thought of it later, though it was really a hundred voices talking over each other on one frequency, each of them calm the way pilots are trained to be calm, and the calm was the worst part. *Mayday, mayday, mayday. Engine fire. Smoke in the cockpit. Electrical. Unable.* Call signs she knew and call signs she didn't. A Southwest out of Omaha. A cargo heavy at forty-one thousand. A Cessna somewhere with a man in it who forgot to say his call sign at all and just kept saying, *it's on fire, it's on fire, the panel is on fire.*

The controller tried for about a minute. Then he stopped assigning anything and just said, over and over, between the carriers, "All aircraft, land at your discretion. All aircraft, land at your discretion." It took her a moment to understand what he meant, which was: *I can't help you. Nobody can help you. Pick a piece of ground.*

She looked out her side window and saw a light falling.

It was far to the south, a white point drawing a line down the dark half of the sky. Too slow for a meteor. Too bright for anything else. It left a trail behind it that held its shape for a long time in the still upper air, and before it reached the horizon she saw a second one, closer, coming apart.

"Ray."

"Don't look at it." His voice was very level. "Look at the panel. Fly the airplane."

---

The left engine caught at 7:52.

The warning bell went, and the red handle lit, and through the side window she could see the back of the nacelle throwing a fan of white sparks into the slipstream, fine and dense as a sparkler. Titanium, she thought, from some lecture years ago, a man in a sweater talking about compressor fires. *Once titanium starts burning in an engine, you don't put it out. You let it finish.*

They pulled the handle. They fired the first bottle, then the second. The sparks thinned and then came back. Ray shut off the fuel and the sparks kept coming anyway, because the engine was not burning fuel anymore. It was burning itself.

At 7:58 the right engine's temperature began to climb.

"How far to Grand Island?"

She had already looked. That was the thing about training: your hands looked before you asked them to. "Ninety-four miles."

"We're at thirty-four thousand." Ray did the arithmetic out loud, the old way, the way you do it when you don't trust the box to do it for you. "Clean, best glide, we've got a hundred, hundred and ten. If the right one goes, we glide."

"And if it doesn't go?"

He looked at the needle climbing. He didn't answer.

They shut the right engine down themselves at 8:03, before it could catch, and the airplane went quiet in a way she had never heard an airplane go quiet. The wind noise rose to fill it. The hydraulics bled away. When the controls went into manual reversion the yoke turned heavy in her hands, heavy as a car door, and she had to brace a foot against the pedestal to hold the nose where it needed to be.

The screens went black one after another. The little standby instruments in the middle of the panel stayed on, running on the battery, and she flew on those: an airspeed needle, an attitude ball, an altimeter unwinding a thousand feet every forty seconds.

"Don't start the APU," Ray said. "It's an engine too."

---

Behind the cockpit door a hundred and sixty people were finding out that the oxygen masks had dropped and that the cabin lights were going and that the woman in 14C was right, there was a fire on the wing, and that their phones would not hold a signal. Maya could hear them through the door, not words, just the rise and fall of it. A child crying. A man laughing, high and wrong.

The lead flight attendant came on the interphone. Her name was Corinne and she had been flying for thirty years. "Talk to me."

"Both engines are shut down," Ray said. "We're gliding into Grand Island. Brace on my call. Corinne—"

"I know."

"There's a lot of them," he said. "Out there. It's not just us."

There was a pause on the line. "Someone back here has a message from his sister in Lisbon. Same thing. Planes coming down. It's everywhere, Ray. It happened everywhere at once."

---

At about twenty past eight, something changed.

She didn't notice it at first, because there was nothing to notice except an absence. The sparks off the left engine had been falling away for some minutes and now they stopped. The smell was still there, but it wasn't getting worse. The standby airspeed needle, which had been trembling, steadied. Out to the south, the falling lights were fewer. Not gone. Fewer.

"Is it me," Ray said, "or did it just—"

"It eased off."

"What did?"

She had no answer. Neither did anyone else, then or for a very long time.

---

Grand Island came up out of the dark as a scatter of lights with a black hole in the middle where the runway should have been lit and wasn't. There was no tower on the frequency. There was no frequency. Maya found the runway the way pilots found runways before any of it, by the shape of the dark: the long straight edge of nothing between the hangar lights and the road.

She had never landed an airliner with no engines and she would never land anything again. She didn't know the second part yet. She knew the first part well enough to be terrified, and the terror was a cold, small, useful thing, like a stone in her pocket.

They dropped the gear by hand, by gravity, Ray hauling on the handle with both arms. Three thumps. No lights to tell them it was down and locked. Corinne's voice came through the door, calm and enormous: *Brace, brace, brace. Heads down, stay down.*

She came in high and fast because high and fast was the only thing she could fix. She slipped it, crossing the controls, the heavy yoke fighting her the whole way, and the dark edge of the runway swam up at her, and then the mains hit, and bounced, and hit again and stayed.

No reversers. The brakes on the accumulator, a few hard applications and then nothing. They ran off the far end at forty knots and through a fence and into a field of cut soybean stubble, and the airplane plowed and slewed and stopped.

For a second there was no sound at all.

Then Ray said, "Evacuate, evacuate, evacuate," and the cabin came alive behind them.

---

They got everyone off. The slides went out with their bang and hiss, and the people came down them into the stubble and ran, the way they had been told, away from the airplane. Corinne was the last of the crew out of the cabin. Ray was last of all.

Maya stood in the field with a hundred and sixty strangers and looked back at the 737. The left engine was still smoking. The rest of it sat there in the starlight, whole, its skin pale and dull in a way she couldn't quite put a name to, as if it had aged ten years on the way down.

A fire truck was coming across the airfield from the terminal side. It was coming slowly, much too slowly, and there was smoke coming out from under it. Halfway across, it stopped. The crew climbed down and ran the rest of the way on foot.

People were holding up their phones to the sky. Most of the screens were dark already. One man was shaking his and shouting at it.

Maya looked up.

The sky over Nebraska was very clear, the way it gets in October when the dust has settled out of the air. The stars were coming out. And all across it, south and east and north, near and far, were the long white trails of the ones that hadn't made it, hanging in the high still air, slowly coming apart. There were too many to count. She tried anyway. She got to thirty and stopped.

Ray came and stood next to her. He still had the paper cup of almonds in his hand. He didn't seem to know he was holding it.

"Good landing," he said.

"It was a terrible landing."

"Everyone walked away from it." He looked up, the way she was looking. "That makes it the best one tonight."

She wanted to say something about tomorrow, about the airline and the investigation and when they'd fly again. She opened her mouth to say it, and found that she couldn't. Some part of her had already understood, standing in that field with the smell of burnt titanium drifting over the stubble, that there would not be a tomorrow like that. That the thing in the air, whatever it was, was not finished.

Nobody flew again. Not out of Grand Island, not out of anywhere. Within a month the fleet was a field of dull grey hulls at every airport in the country. Within a year, the stories had already begun.

---

*In the south, where the Kathā are still told, they say that in the first hour of the Rustfall the flying machines of the old world caught fire in the sky, all of them at once, and fell burning like the stars at the end of an age. That is not quite true. Some of them came down whole. Somebody had to fly the last ones home.*

# Dead Hand

*A Rustfall Kathā*

Oct 20, 2026 · @Kristoffer Law

Object Sever had been built to outlive the world, and for most of a night it believed that it had.

It sat two hundred metres down in the granite of the Kola Peninsula, under a hill of old iron workings, a backup node for a system so old that the men who had designed it were mostly dead. The system had a long official name nobody used. The crews called it the Old Man. Western newspapers, years ago, had called it the Dead Hand, and the crews had laughed at that, and then stopped laughing, because the newspapers had more or less understood it.

The idea was simple. If the country's leaders were killed in a first strike, and the war's first hour left no one alive to give the order, the Old Man would give it for them. It listened for the signs: the silence of the command links, the tremor of the ground, the counters ticking up. It did not need anyone to decide. It needed only to be told, at regular intervals, by a working link from Moscow, that the world was still there. If that message stopped coming while the signs said war, the Old Man would assume the worst and act on it.

Lieutenant Colonel Andrei Voronin had been on duty at Sever for six years and had never once seen the Old Man do anything but wait.

---

At 03:41 Moscow time nothing happened.

At 03:46 Senior Lieutenant Pasha Ilyin, who was twenty-six and had the communications desk, said, "Something's burning."

They all smelled it then. Hot metal and electrical char, the smell of a fuse box after a short. Voronin sent the duty warrant officer, Sokol, to check the ventilation plant. Sokol was fifty-eight and had worked on the Old Man's cabinets since he was nineteen, and he went down the corridor at his usual pace, which was the pace of a man who has seen a great many alarms come to nothing.

He came back at a run.

"The fans," he said. "The bearings. They're smoking. All of them at once."

The lights flickered. On Ilyin's desk the modern consoles, the ones installed nine years ago with the new encrypted links to Moscow, began to throw error codes Voronin had never seen. The screens filled and cleared and filled again. Down the long room the status boards blinked from green to amber in no order that made any sense.

And in its own steel cabinets along the far wall, the Old Man woke up.

It woke because the early-warning radar at Olenegorsk, forty kilometres away, had just reported a mass launch from the Barents Sea.

---

"It's false," Voronin said. "It has to be false."

The tracks on the screen were absurd: dozens, then hundreds, appearing and vanishing in clusters, some of them moving backwards. A radar dying, not a radar seeing. He knew it. Ilyin knew it. But the Old Man did not know anything; it only counted, and the count had crossed a threshold set forty years ago by men who had imagined every way the world could end except this one. A relay closed somewhere in the cabinets with a sound like a knuckle cracking. The combat-mode lamp came on, a round red light the size of a coin.

"Get me Moscow."

Ilyin tried. The encrypted link connected, and dropped, and connected, and dropped. When it held for long enough to carry voice, the voice on the other end was a duty officer at the General Staff, and he was shouting, and what he was shouting was that their own screens were full of tracks too, and their own lights were going, and did Sever see what they were seeing, did anyone see it, was it real.

"Send the hold," Voronin said. "Send the hold signal now, keep sending it. The Old Man is armed."

"We're sending it."

The hold came through. The counter on the Old Man's panel, which had begun to run, stopped and reset. Voronin found that he had been gripping the edge of the desk hard enough to hurt.

The hold came through again, forty minutes later. And again. And then, at 05:20, it didn't.

---

At about twenty to five, something eased.

It was Sokol who noticed. He had been standing at the ventilation hatch with his hand on the duct, feeling for the fans, and he said, "It's slowing down." Not stopping. Slowing. The burning smell was no worse than it had been ten minutes before. The amber lights stopped spreading. For a while the men in the long room dared to think it was over: a storm of some kind, a solar flare, an attack on the grid, something that had come and was going.

It was not over. It had only stopped getting worse. Everything that had been damaged in that first hour stayed damaged, and kept on failing at its own pace, and the pace was fast.

By six the encrypted link was gone for good. By seven the backup satellite terminal was dead; the dish on the surface could no longer find anything to talk to, though the satellites themselves, had anyone been able to ask them, were perfectly well. By eight the main generators had shut themselves down when their control computers failed, and the bunker ran on two old diesels from 1986 that had no computers at all and started on the first try.

"Thank God for junk," Sokol said.

He said the same thing about the Old Man, an hour later, and then he didn't say it again.

---

The Old Man was built from the same junk.

Voronin had always known that, the way you know a fact about your own house without ever thinking about it. The cabinets were Soviet work, designed when the country still made its own components and made them to last: relays with gold-plated contacts, switches sealed in glass, circuit boards stuffed with gold and palladium that scrap dealers in the nineties had paid good money to strip. Everything modern in the bunker was rotting in front of them. The Old Man's cabinets were cool and clean and steady, and their little lamps glowed exactly as they had glowed for forty years.

And the counter on its panel was running again.

The hold signal had to be renewed. That was the design, the whole design: silence meant death. The hold came from Moscow down the encrypted link, and the encrypted link was dead, and there was no other way to send it. Someone had decided, nine years ago, that the old hardline from the Brezhnev days was obsolete, and had pulled its terminal equipment out to make room for the new consoles.

"How long?" Voronin asked.

Sokol read the counter. He did not need to look anything up. "Eleven hours. Maybe a bit less. It starts the final sequence when the sensors confirm."

"There's nothing for them to confirm. Nobody has hit anything."

Sokol turned and looked at the sensor board.

The seismic channels were moving. Little tremors, coming and going, nothing like the shock of a warhead, but enough. The radiation counters were ticking up too, slowly, in jumps, the way counters tick when their amplifiers are dying and every burst of electrical noise looks like a particle. Behind the bunker's walls the hill above them was full of old iron, the ore bodies the Soviets had dug for decades, and Voronin did not know yet, nobody knew yet, that the thing in the air was worse over iron than anywhere else.

"The sensors are lying," he said.

"Yes," said Sokol. "And the Old Man believes them. That's what it's for."

---

They tried everything.

Ilyin tried every radio in the bunker. Most were dead. One shortwave set still worked, intermittently, and he sat with it for hours calling Moscow, calling the Northern Fleet at Severomorsk, calling anyone. Severomorsk answered once, for about ninety seconds, and the officer on the line said the submarines at the piers had lost their electronics, that the airfield was full of burning aircraft, that he had no orders and no one to ask. Then he was gone.

Sokol and two conscripts went for the cable vault, where the Old Man's lines ran out to the silo of its command rocket a kilometre away. If they could cut the lines, the rocket could not be told to go. The vault was behind a blast door, and the door moved on hydraulics, and the hydraulic pumps had been running all night to keep pressure, and the pumps were seized solid. They tried to open the door by hand with the emergency crank. The crank's gearing had been turning, a little, every time the bunker flexed with the cold, and it had rusted to its housing in a single night. Sokol broke two pry bars on it and wept with anger, and nobody pretended not to see.

Voronin went to the Old Man's cabinets with a fire axe and stood in front of them for a long time.

He knew the design. Everyone at Sever knew it. You could not stop the Old Man by breaking it. Break the cabinet and the backup cabinet at another node, three hundred kilometres away, would see the break as one more sign of attack. Kill the power and the batteries held. Cut the sensors and the missing sensors counted as confirmation. The system had been built by men who were afraid of exactly this: of a frightened colonel with an axe, at the end of the world, trying to stop the machine from doing the thing it had been built to do.

He put the axe down.

---

The light came up outside at nine. Voronin went to the surface to see it, because he wanted to see something that was not a failing screen. The sky over the Kola was clear and pale, and the snow on the hill was very white, and the vehicles in the motor pool were the colour of dried blood. The steel of the blast door, which had been grey and painted the evening before, had a bloom on it like mould on bread.

He thought: this is not a weapon. Nobody could make a weapon that did this to everything at once, their own side included.

He went back down and said so, and Ilyin said, "Then what is it?"

Voronin didn't know. Nobody in the world knew, then, and nobody would ever know for certain.

---

At 15:52 the counter reached zero.

The Old Man did not hesitate, because it could not. The final sequence ran in a few seconds: relays closing in order down the long cabinets, click, click, click, the lamps changing from amber to red one after another like the windows of a train going by at night.

They felt the rocket go through the rock. A long, deep vibration in the floor, rising and rising, and then fading as it climbed away. It was not a weapon. It carried no warhead at all. It carried a transmitter, and as it rose over the peninsula, out of the shadow of the hills, it began to broadcast the order the Old Man had been built to give, on frequencies the silos far to the south were still alive to hear.

The silos were further south. Their electronics had more time left. They were working perfectly.

---

At 16:20 Ilyin's shortwave set picked up a voice.

It was faint and it was in English, then Norwegian, then English again: a fishing boat off Vardø, a hundred kilometres west. The skipper was calling anyone. His engine was on fire. His radio was dying. His crew had found their knives rusted in the galley drawer. Was anyone hearing this. Was it happening anywhere else. Was this a war.

Ilyin keyed the microphone. He was crying, Voronin saw, the way the young cry, with no sound. He said, in his bad English, "Yes. Everywhere. It is everywhere. It is not a war."

The skipper said, "Thank God." Then his set went quiet.

Voronin sat down in the duty chair in front of the Old Man's cabinets, whose lamps were all red now, steady and clean, and waited with his hands in his lap for the rest of the world to answer.

---

*In the north, those who are left say the south did this to them. In the south, where the Kathā are told, they say the north burned the world. Both are partly right. There is an old story, told in both places, about a king who died in the night, and whose hand went on striking after he was gone, because no one remained who could tell it to stop.*

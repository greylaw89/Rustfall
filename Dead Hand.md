# The Old Man and the Dead King

*A Rustfall Kathā*

Oct 20, 2026 · @Kristoffer Law

Object Sever. Kola Peninsula. Two hundred metres of granite, under a hill of old iron workings. Backup node, second category. Duty commander: Lieutenant Colonel A. V. Voronin, six years in post. Incidents in six years: none.

Voronin liked that line in the log. *Incidents: none.* It was the only line that mattered.

The system Sever served had an official designation nine words long. The crews called it the Old Man. It did one thing. It waited for a message from Moscow, at fixed intervals, saying that the country's command still existed. While the message came, the Old Man slept. If the message stopped, and its sensors said the country was under attack, it would launch the order to retaliate itself, with no one's permission, because there would be no one left to give it.

The Western papers had called it the Dead Hand. Voronin considered that imprecise. A dead hand does nothing. The Old Man was a hand that went on working after the head was gone.

---

03:41. Nothing.

03:46. Senior Lieutenant Ilyin, communications, twenty-six, says: "Something's burning."

Voronin smells it. Electrical. Hot metal. A fuse box after a short. He sends Warrant Officer Sokol to the ventilation plant. Sokol is fifty-eight. Forty years on the Old Man's cabinets. Walks like a man who has seen every alarm on the base come to nothing.

Sokol comes back running. Voronin notes that. Sokol has not run in six years.

"The fans. Bearings smoking. All of them. At once."

"All?"

"All."

Lights flicker. The modern consoles on Ilyin's desk begin to throw error codes. Installed nine years ago with the new encrypted link to Moscow. Voronin knows the manual for those consoles by heart. He does not know these codes. Status boards down the long room go green to amber, in no order. No order is the worst sign. Faults have an order. Attacks have an order.

At 03:52 a relay closes in the Old Man's cabinets with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

The radar at Olenegorsk, forty kilometres, has reported a mass launch from the Barents Sea.

---

The tracks are wrong. He sees it at once. Hundreds, appearing and vanishing in clusters, some moving backwards. That is not a radar seeing. That is a radar dying. He knows it. Ilyin knows it.

The Old Man does not know anything. It counts. The count has crossed a threshold set forty years ago. The combat-mode lamp comes on. Round, red, the size of a kopeck.

"Moscow."

Ilyin tries. The link connects, drops, connects, drops. When it holds, the duty officer at the General Staff is shouting. Their screens are full of tracks too. Their lights are going. Is Sever seeing it. Is anyone. Is it real.

"Send the hold. Keep sending it. The Old Man is armed."

"Sending."

The hold comes through. The counter on the Old Man's panel stops and resets. Voronin finds he has been holding the edge of the desk. He lets go. He notes the time.

04:31: hold received.

05:11: hold received.

05:20: hold not received.

---

At about 04:40, something had eased. He writes it down afterwards, because he did not write it down at the time and the omission bothers him. The fans stopped getting worse. The smell held steady. The amber stopped spreading. For twenty minutes the long room believed it was over. A flare. Sabotage. Something with an end.

It was not over. It had stopped accelerating. Everything damaged in the first hour stayed damaged and kept failing on its own schedule, and the schedule was fast.

06:00: encrypted link lost. Not restored.

07:00: satellite terminal dead. The dish on the surface cannot find anything to talk to. The satellites themselves, he will learn much later, were perfectly well.

08:00: main generators shut down. Control computers failed. Bunker on the two reserve diesels. Manufactured 1986. No computers. Both start first try.

Sokol, watching them run: "Thank God for junk."

He said it again an hour later, about the Old Man. Then he stopped saying it.

---

The Old Man was the same junk.

Voronin had known that for six years without once thinking it through. Soviet cabinets. Built when the country made its own parts and made them to last. Relays with gold-plated contacts. Switches sealed in glass. Boards that scrap dealers in the nineties had stripped for the gold and palladium in them. Everything new in the bunker was rotting in front of him. The Old Man's cabinets were cool and clean, and every lamp glowed exactly as it had for forty years.

The counter was running again.

The design was simple. Silence meant death. The hold came from Moscow down the encrypted link. The encrypted link was dead. There was no second path. The old Brezhnev-era hardline had been decommissioned nine years ago and its terminal removed to make room for the new consoles. He had signed the decommissioning order himself. It had been a sound decision. He could cite the regulation.

"How long," he said.

Sokol did not look anything up. "Eleven hours. Bit less. Final sequence starts when the sensors confirm."

"Nothing has hit anything."

Sokol looked at the sensor board.

Seismic channels: moving. Small tremors, nothing like a warhead, enough to register. Radiation counters: rising in jumps, the way counters rise when their amplifiers are dying and every burst of noise reads as a particle. And the hill above them full of old ore, the iron the Soviets dug for decades. Voronin did not know yet that the thing in the air was worse over iron. Nobody knew yet.

"The sensors are lying."

"Yes," said Sokol. "And the Old Man believes them. That's what it's for."

---

Measures taken, in order.

One. Radio. Ilyin tries every set in the bunker. Most dead. One shortwave set works, on and off. He calls Moscow, the Northern Fleet, anyone. Severomorsk answers once, for about ninety seconds: the submarines at the piers have lost their electronics, the airfield is full of burning aircraft, no orders, no one to ask. Then nothing.

Two. The cable vault. The Old Man's lines run to the silo of its command rocket a kilometre away. Cut the lines and the rocket cannot be told to go. Sokol and two conscripts go for the vault. Blast door on hydraulics. Hydraulic pumps have run all night holding pressure. Pumps seized. Emergency crank: its gearing has moved a little every time the bunker flexed with the cold, and it has rusted solid to its housing in one night. Sokol breaks two pry bars on it. He weeps with anger. No one pretends not to see.

Three. Voronin takes the fire axe to the Old Man's cabinets and stands in front of them for some time.

He knows the design. Break the cabinet: the backup node three hundred kilometres away reads the break as one more sign of attack. Cut the power: batteries. Cut the sensors: missing sensors count as confirmation. The system was built by men afraid of exactly this. A frightened colonel with an axe, at the end of the world, trying to stop it doing the thing it was built to do.

He puts the axe back on its bracket. He logs it.

---

09:00. Surface, to see the light. The sky over the Kola is pale and clear. Snow on the hill. The vehicles in the motor pool are the colour of dried blood. The blast door, grey and painted at 03:00, has a bloom on it like mould on bread.

This is not a weapon, he thinks. No one could build a weapon that did this to everything at once, their own side included.

He goes down and says so. Ilyin says, "Then what is it?"

He has no answer. Nobody in the world had one then. Nobody would ever have one for certain.

---

15:52. Counter: zero.

The Old Man does not hesitate. It cannot. The final sequence takes seconds. Relays closing down the long cabinets, click, click, click. Lamps going amber to red one after another, like the windows of a train passing at night.

They feel the rocket through the rock. A long low vibration rising in the floor, then fading as it climbs away. It is not a weapon. It carries no warhead. It carries a transmitter. As it clears the hills it begins to broadcast the order the Old Man was built to give, on frequencies the silos far to the south are still alive to hear.

The silos are further south. Their electronics have more time. They are working perfectly.

---

16:20. Ilyin's shortwave set picks up a voice.

Faint. English, then Norwegian, then English. A fishing boat off Vardø, a hundred kilometres west. The skipper is calling anyone. Engine on fire. Radio dying. His crew found their knives rusted in the galley drawer. Is anyone hearing this. Is it happening anywhere else. Is this a war.

Ilyin keys the microphone. He is crying, Voronin sees, the way the young cry, with no sound. In his bad English: "Yes. Everywhere. It is everywhere. It is not a war."

The skipper says, "Thank God." His set goes quiet.

Voronin opens the log. He writes the time, and *Incidents:*, and stops.

Then he sits down in the duty chair in front of the Old Man's cabinets, whose lamps are all red now, steady and clean, and waits with his hands in his lap for the rest of the world to answer.

---

*In the north, those who are left say the south did this to them. In the south, where the Kathā are told, they say the north burned the world. Both are partly right. There is an old story, told in both places, about a king who died in the night, and whose hand went on striking after he was gone, because no one remained who could tell it to stop.*

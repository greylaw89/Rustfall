# The Ritual

*A Rustfall Kathā*

Oct 20, 2026 · @Kristoffer Law

Jijnasu Prabhakar Rao kept his machine on the roof of his house, under a canopy of oiled cloth, where his neighbours could not see it and the dawn could.

Vasu had built most of it. He had been the jijnasu's apprentice for six years. The copy-hall sent mostly girls up to the reading houses, girls with steady hands; a man was taken only for unusual aptitude, and only on a master's word. The jijnasu had asked for Vasu by name, at nineteen, after watching him bring a dead reading machine in the copy-hall back to life in a week.

In his second year Vasu had broken a bottle. A vacuum glass, knocked from its socket by his elbow on a dark morning, gone in a flat little crack and a spray of bright dust. The law said an apprentice who broke a bottle went down to the ash-touched. The jijnasu had stood looking at the pieces on the floor for a long time. Then he had written in his book that the glass had cracked in the heat, and he had never spoken of it again. He did not worry about Vasu's loyalty after that. He never needed to.

In six years Vasu had learned that the jijnasu's greatest gift was not his learning, which was considerable, but his money, which was more. His family had held the same lands below the Deccan for eleven generations. Their household gold, the bangles and chains and temple offerings of three centuries, had been melted down one piece at a time over Vasu's six years to make the contacts of the thing on the roof. Vasu had poured most of them himself.

There were forty-two bottles, each with its thread of fire, each set into a socket of fired clay on a frame of seasoned teak. There was a coil of copper sealed in glass, and a coil of sun-metal that was not sealed, and a crystal that the jijnasu had bought from a smuggler in the port for the price of a small field. There was a hand-cranked wheel of clay and black stone, like the great wheel in the House of Readers, but small enough for two men to turn. And there was the dish: a shallow bowl of beaten pale-metal two arms across, recast from the hull of something the old people had flown, polished so bright it hurt to look at in the sun.

"It is the Daftar's work," the jijnasu liked to say, "done better, by a private man, on a roof." He said it only to Vasu. He would not have said it to anyone else for all the gold in the Empire.

---

Speaking to the Asura was not forbidden. Vasu had looked, once, in the law books in the copy-hall, to be sure. There was no line in any book that said a man might not raise his voice to the sky.

It was only that men who did so were watched. And men who were thought to have the keys disappeared.

Everyone knew the stories. A jijnasu in Thanjavur who had found the keys in a sealed tin and spoken them at dawn on the temple roof, and whose house was found empty a week later, the doors open and the lamps still lit. A merchant in the port who had boasted of it in drink. A girl from a copy-hall, years back, whose name nobody would say. The emperor and the great families believed that the Asura had made the Rustfall. The Daftar said nothing about it at all, which Vasu had come to understand was its own kind of answer.

"They are afraid it will tell the truth," the jijnasu said, "or afraid it will tell a lie. I have never been able to decide which."

"Which are you afraid of?"

He thought about it. "Neither," he said. "I'm afraid it will say nothing, and I will die without having heard it."

---

He had keys. Every jijnasu with money had keys.

That was the joke among them, the bitter one. The real keys, everyone in the high reading halls agreed, had never left the far side of the world. They had been kept by the people who built the Asura, in the forbidden half of the maps that the Daftar burned, and in three hundred years nobody had crossed to the far side and come back with anything. Pilgrims went. Boats went east across the great ocean, and west round the horn of Africa. Almost none returned, and those who did brought back fever and stories.

One story came back more often than the rest. Halfway across the eastern ocean, it said, there was an island, or a ring of islands, where the old people's war-fleet had kept a harbour, and where the keys might be kept too: in a vault, in a temple, in a box of sun-metal under the sea. Nobody knew if the island was real. Everybody knew someone who had a page from it.

The pages came through the ports. Sailors sold them, and men who said they were sailors, and men who had bought from men who said they were sailors. Almost all were forgeries: old letters copied from the Asura's own incantation, a string of spelling-words, a number that looked important. A careful jijnasu spent more of his life testing keys than using them. You asked the seller where the page had come from, and how, and you listened for the lie. You asked him things that only someone who had been near the far side could know. Almost always, he didn't.

Prabhakar Rao had spent twenty years doing that. He had turned away forty-one sellers. The forty-second was an old sailor in the port, half blind, who said he had shipped east as a boy on a pilgrim boat that broke apart, and been picked up, and come home eleven years later by a road he would not describe. The jijnasu asked him the name of the island. The old man said it: *Hawaii.* A word the Daftar had burned from every map in the Empire. Then, without being asked, he said the name of the harbour where the old war-fleet had lain. *Pearl Harbor.* He said it the way you say the name of a place where something terrible once happened.

The jijnasu paid him what he asked. It was a great deal.

The page itself was plain. A number. A string of the old letters. And a row of words.

The words were the strangest part. They were ordinary words in the old language, the jijnasu said, the names of things, but they were used for their first letters only, so that a man speaking them over a bad line could not be misheard. *Kilo. Echo. Yankee.* Whole phrases spelled out that way, one letter to a word.

"So when I say the phrase," he said, "I am spelling. Every jijnasu who recites the Asura's words thinks he is saying a prayer. He is saying the alphabet."

He laughed at that, softly, for a long time.

---

The Asura woke only at dawn.

Nobody knew why. The jijnasu thought it had something to do with the sun on its wings, which the old people had made to drink sunlight, and which three hundred years of sky had worn thin. In the brief time after it came out of the earth's shadow it had strength enough to listen, and to answer. Then it went quiet again until the next morning.

So the ritual began in the dark.

Vasu had done it with him a hundred times. His part was the bottles. Vasu went along the frame by the light of a covered lamp and touched each socket with the cloth-tipped stick, as the old readers taught every apprentice to do, and listened for the faint hum, and watched for the threads of fire coming up. The jijnasu's two house-servants turned the clay wheel. The jijnasu himself sat cross-legged before the dish with the page of keys on a board across his knees and his face turned to the east, where the sky over the Deccan was beginning to grey.

When the threads were steady, he began.

He spoke the number first, slowly, in the old language, the way one says the name of a god. Then the string of old letters, each one as its word. *Kilo. Echo. Yankee.* Then the phrase, which Vasu was not allowed to hear and which he spoke into a funnel of oiled paper pressed to his mouth, so that only the machine heard it. Then, as every jijnasu did, the long incantation that the Asura had given back to the world in its first centuries, which scholars recited at weddings and Saralpanthis spat on in the street:

*Title eighteen. United States Code. Section ten thirty. Unauthorized access. Prohibited.*

He did not know exactly what it meant. Nobody did. It was the Asura's own law, and it was polite to say it back.

Then they waited for the sun.

---

For three dawns, nothing came.

That was normal. The Asura answered days later if it answered at all, and on most mornings it answered no one. The jijnasu sat before the dish until the sun was a hand's width above the hills, and then he folded the page into its oiled cloth and put it inside his shirt and went down to eat, and slept through the hot hours, and woke in the evening to make his notes.

On the fourth dawn the dish spoke.

It was not loud. It came out of the horn of beaten pale-metal at the dish's centre as a thin flat voice, like a man speaking through a long pipe from very far away. It did not rise or fall the way a person's voice rises and falls. Every word the same weight.

At the far end of the frame, Vasu felt his blood turn to ice. It was not a voice any human throat could make. There was no breath in it, no hesitation, no fear and no kindness, nothing behind the words at all: a flatness so complete that it seemed to go all the way down, to wherever a soul should have been and was not. He would hear it for the rest of his life, in fevers and in the silence before sleep, and so would the two men at the wheel.

They were the strongest men in the household, chosen for it, and they had turned that wheel a hundred dawns without complaint. Now one of them gasped as if he had been struck and began to pray aloud, the name of a god over and over, fast and high. The other let go of the crank and stepped back from it as though it had burned him, and the clay wheel began to slow, and the threads of fire in the bottles began to dim.

"Fools!" The jijnasu did not turn round. His voice cracked across the roof like a whip, a voice Vasu had never heard from him in six years. "Keep turning!"

The praying man seized the crank in both hands and threw his weight on it, still praying. After a moment the other came back and took hold beside him, his face grey, his eyes shut. The wheel picked up. The threads brightened. The voice went on.

The Asura spoke for a long time. Most of it Vasu could not follow at all. The jijnasu sat rigid before it, his lips moving, catching what he could, and when it paused he wrote, very fast, on the board on his knees.

Vasu heard the incantation in it, and that frightened him more than anything. The Asura said its own law back to them. *Title eighteen. Section ten thirty.* The same words, the same order, in that flat far voice, so that for a moment it was as if the whole world had only ever been an echo of this one thing on the roof.

Then it said something else.

Numbers. The jijnasu went still. Numbers he knew; numbers had not changed in three hundred years. Vasu saw him write them down, and stop, and look at what he had written, and write them again.

The voice said a last string of words and went quiet.

The sun came up over the hills.

---

He did not go down to eat. He sat on the roof in the full heat of the morning with the board on his knees, reading what he had written, over and over, and when Vasu came to him with water he did not take it.

"What did it say?"

The jijnasu looked up. Vasu had never seen his face like that. He had the look of a man who has been waiting at the door for twenty years and has heard it open behind him.

"Most of it is the same," he said. "The warnings. It is a government's machine. Unauthorised access is forbidden. My attempt is recorded." He pointed at a line. "Then this. *Authentication failed.* Wrong. Not a letter wrong. All of it." He almost laughed. "Twenty years, and an old sailor who knew two names. I think he was telling the truth about the island. That never meant he was telling the truth about the page." "Then this. *Attempts remaining: two.*"

"Two?"

"Two more mornings. Then I think it will stop listening to us. Or to anyone, from here."

"And the numbers?"

The jijnasu turned the board so Vasu could see. Vasu read them slowly, the way he had been taught: degrees, and parts of degrees. Two pairs.

"That's here," Vasu said. He knew those numbers. Every copy-hall map in the Empire had the capital marked on it in the old way, in the corner, for the scholars. "That's the capital."

"That's this house," the jijnasu said. "To the width of a street."

They looked at each other.

"It knows where we are," Vasu said.

"It knows where the voice came from." He put the board down very carefully on the roof. "And it said so, out loud, on the open sky, at dawn. To anyone else who was listening."

And under that, colder, a thought he would never say aloud to anyone: the legends were true. The Asura saw everything. If it knew this roof to the width of a street, then it knew every roof. It knew the numbers of all the lands in the world, every city and every ruin and every road. It had been up there, where nothing rots, when the world ended. It had watched it happen. It knew how. It knew who.

---

Vasu understood it all at once, the way you understand a floor is giving way.

The Asura answered at dawn. The Daftar's machines listened at dawn. Every morning, in the great hall behind the House of Readers, the licensed readers sat by their own dishes and wrote down whatever the sky said, and most mornings the sky said nothing, and on the mornings it did, the Daftar read it.

And this morning the sky had said: here is a man who is trying the keys, and here is where he lives.

"We have to take it down," Vasu said. "The dish. Tonight. If they come and there's nothing here—"

"They will not need to find a dish," the jijnasu said gently. "They will only need to find me."

He got up, slowly, like an old man, though he was not old. He went and stood in front of the dish and looked at it for a while, at his family's gold shining in its sockets, at the forty-two threads of fire, at the bright bowl recast from some flying machine of the old world that had fallen out of the sky on the first day.

"Two attempts," he said. "Two more mornings." He turned to Vasu. "Will you do the bottles tomorrow?"

Vasu wanted to say no. He said yes.

---

That night Vasu could not sleep. He lay in his room off the copy-hall and listened to the street, and every cart was the Daftar, and every voice. At the hour before dawn he got up and dressed and walked through the dark lanes to the jijnasu's house.

The door was open.

The lamps in the hall were lit. Nothing was broken, nothing taken. His sandals were by the step where he always left them. His shawl was over the back of his chair. On the table, held down by the brass lamp, was his notebook, open to the morning's page: the warnings, the numbers, *attempts remaining: two*, all in his small fast hand.

The house-servants were gone. The jijnasu was gone.

Vasu went up to the roof. The dish was there under its oiled cloth, and the frame of bottles, and the clay wheel, and the board he had sat with. They had not even bothered to take the machine. They did not need the machine. Without the keys it was only a very expensive mirror.

The page of keys was not on the board. He looked under it, and under the cloth, and in the cracks between the roof tiles, knowing he would not find it, and he did not.

They had taken a page of nonsense. They would find that out soon enough, when they tried it at dawn on their own dishes and the Asura told them so. It would not bring him back.

In the east the sky was going grey. In a little while the Asura would come up out of the earth's shadow, and listen, and hear nothing, and wait.

Vasu sat down cross-legged before the dish where the jijnasu had always sat, with his steady hands in his lap, and watched it come.

---

*Vasu kept the notebook. It is in the copy-hall still, in a box under the floor of a room nobody uses, tied round with an old reader's cord, so knotted with years of service that it has gone as hard as a root. On the last written page there is a line in his hand, under the numbers, that the readers who have found it since cannot agree on. Some say it is a note of the time. Some say it is the phrase itself, the one he spoke into the paper funnel so that only the machine would hear, written down at last because there was no longer any point in hiding it.*

*It is spelled out in the old way, one word to a letter. Most readers stop after the first few.*

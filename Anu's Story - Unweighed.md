# Unweighed

*A Rustfall Kathā*

Oct 20, 2026 · @Kristoffer Law

Six jars. The weigher wrote them in his book on the second morning in the capital, and Anu watched his pen go, though she could only read the numbers. The best one had her name by it. Meera's doing.

That night Meera took her down to the river steps.

The stone was still warm. Up in the lodging-house the others were drinking to six good jars and nobody dead. Meera had a lamp with her and didn't light it. Anu sat where she was told and kept her knees together, then remembered and let them fall apart, the way a boy sits.

"You can stop that," Meera said. "Here. With me."

Anu didn't stop. Her body didn't know how to anymore.

"The glass," Meera said. "You've been thinking about it."

Anu said nothing. Saying nothing was safest. It was the first thing she'd learned at the sheds.

"So have I. Seventy-seven, in one line. You'd have walked out free." Meera looked at the water. "And in a week a man from the Daftar would have come to the sheds to see the boy one glass had freed. And he'd have looked at your face. And your hands. As long as he liked." She said it flat. Meera said everything flat. "Tahir saw that before I did. That's why it isn't in the jars."

"I know."

"Do you know why I know?"

Anu looked at her.

"Because I was you," Meera said. "Twenty-six years ago. Other hall. Other crew. I got through my seven before anyone looked close, and then the law stopped asking." She shrugged. "I was lucky. Seven years of lucky. You won't get that many. Most girls don't. I'm not going to sit and watch you try."

Anu knew about Dev. Everybody did. The man who went through the floor, and the baby after, that came too early and didn't breathe. Nobody said it to Meera's face. Anu didn't either. But she thought it, sitting there. She thought: *she is doing this because of them.* And then she thought: *I don't mind.*

---

There was a scribe, Meera said. From the records hall. He came to her in the market two months back. He knew her name because everyone knew it: Meera the saptajit, Meera who beat her seven. He thought a woman who'd done that might understand a thing a man wouldn't.

"He wants a book," Meera said. "Not glass. A book."

Anu almost laughed. Books were the black lumps on the shelves in every ruin. Swollen. Furred. You walked past them. *Touch nothing soft.* Books were mold, and mold was the cough, and the cough was the end.

"Why?"

"I asked him. He said a man with eyes can read a book. Glass needs the Daftar's machines." Meera turned the dark lamp in her hands. "He said a lot I didn't follow. There's a crew report, years old. The old school of the sciences, north of the cold halls. A room with a gold cabinet in it. The book's in the same room, by the window. That crew wrote it down, and then they left it, because the room was black with mold and nobody would put a hand on anything."

"What's in it?"

"Plants. How a leaf eats the light." Meera almost smiled. "That's all I got. He wrote me the word it's about, in the old letters." She took out a slip of palm paper and opened it.

Fourteen shapes. Hooks and rounds and sticks. Anu could read some of the Empire's letters, slowly, a few at a time, the way you pick stones out of rice. These weren't those.

"I can't read that."

"Me neither. You don't read it. You find it. Wherever those shapes are, that's a page he wants. Cut those pages clean. Bring them past the post."

"Then what?"

"Then he makes you a paper." Meera said it slowly, so Anu would get every piece. "Born free. Passed the evaluation at fourteen. A place as an apprentice at a copy-hall. They take girls there. Girls with steady hands. And the same thing written in the files, so if anyone ever looks, the files and the paper say the same." A pause. "He can do it. He says he's done it before. If they caught him he'd be made ash-touched, same as us. So once it's done, it's done. He never talks. You never talk. You'd both lose everything."

Anu sat very still. She could hear the river. She could hear Jai laughing up in the house.

"There's more," Meera said. "The boy has to die."

---

Meera had it worked out, the way she worked out a hall. Step, step, step.

Anu's jars would go to the others. Jai, Ravi, Tahir. So nobody lost by losing her. Two more runs, same as always. The second one back to the cold hall on the Long Shore. The floor at the west end was going. Meera had walked it. The iron rods inside the stone were swelling with rust and cracking it open from inside, like roots in a wall. It would come down soon whether anyone wanted it or not.

"When it comes down, you're on it," Meera said. "As far as anyone sees."

"And they'll look for me."

"No. They won't."

Meera didn't make that part soft. Anu was glad. Nobody went into the mold for a body. A body in a ruin was the mold's own house in three days, four, and whoever went in after it came out coughing. Your crew took your hood off you if they could reach you. Fast. Before. After that, nobody touched you. A crew that lost someone under a floor said the name at the post and walked on. A crew that came a year later would see the hole and the dark under it and make the sign and turn round.

The floor was the proof. Nobody would climb down to check it.

"The crew," Anu said.

"The crew don't want to know." Meera looked up at the bright window. "They're not stupid. Jai looks at your hands. Tahir's known since the first week, I think. But nobody's said it. If nobody says it, nobody has to lie. The less they know, the better." Quiet. "They'll cry for you. That bit's real."

Anu thought about the glass in the slot behind the seventh cabinet, under the moss. Seventy-seven in one line, and a man looking at her face. Or a few pages nobody would weigh. A scribe she'd never see again. A girl born free, on paper.

"Yes," she said.

---

The old school of the sciences was a long way north, low buildings round what had been a garden once and was forest now. The crew thought they'd come for the gold cabinet in the report. They found it. Dry room. Black machines furred bronze-green. Good moss. Meera put Jai and Ravi on the jars and Tahir on the scraping. Then: "Anu. East corridor. Lamp. Look for more cabinets. Touch nothing."

Tahir looked at Meera once. Said nothing. Went back to his knife.

Third room on the left. Like the report said.

The window had no glass. Three hundred years of rain had come in and the mold had come in with it. Black. Grey. A brown like old blood. In sheets. In drifts. The shelves had fallen against each other like trees after a storm, and the books on them had swollen and split and grown together into lumps that used to be rows.

Anu stood in the door with the lamp. *Breathe slow. Breathe through the hood.* She was more scared than she'd ever been in any hall.

By the window. One shelf had fallen out, away from the wet, not into it. The books there were less bad. Spines faded to dust-colour. Whatever iron had held them was long gone, rotted out, so some were just blocks of leaves stuck together with old glue. She knelt without letting her knees touch the floor. She held up the palm paper next to each one in the light. Fourteen shapes. Hook, round, stick. Looking. Looking.

Most of an hour. Fifth book.

There. And there. And there again. Top of the page. Middle of a line. Big, at the start of a part. The book was full of it. The book was *about* it.

Her hand shook. The lamp shook. She made it stop. A shaking lamp is a lamp that falls.

The edges were gone. Mold had eaten in from all round, soft, furred, dark. Brown stains spread from a row of little iron pins rotted away in the spine. But in the middle, where the pages had been pressed tight for three hundred years: yellow. Brittle at the corners. Clean.

She cut them out with her ceramic knife. Meera had shown her on a rag at the lodging-house. *Cut well inside the stain. Never through it. Never let the knife touch the black and then the clean.* Four bundles of leaves. She held each one up to the lamp. One had a grey spot on the inside fold. She put it down and didn't pick it up again. Three good. The shapes in all three, over and over.

She folded them flat. She slit the inside of her tunic under the belt and laid them in against her skin and sewed it shut with the bone needle and the waxed thread. Forty times she'd practised that, in the dark, with her eyes shut. It went fine. Then she stood up and walked out and did not look back at what was left of the book.

---

At the post they burned their outer cloth in the smoke, same as always. The keeper watched. Hands up, same as always. He looked at the scrapes and the nails. He got to Anu and looked at her hand, which didn't shake. Then at her face. Then down at his book. He made his mark.

Three days at the post, watching each other's lips. The second night she dreamed black fur growing over her hands and woke up stiff as wood. She lay still until she could feel the pages against her skin. Dry. Flat. Then she breathed.

In the capital, in the lane behind the lodging-house, Meera took the pages off her without a word and put them inside her own tunic and walked off.

---

Second run. The cold hall.

Same as before. The iron tongue at the post, the year, the long walk over the mounds through the forest, the dark west end that smelled of wet iron, the fig tree coming through the roof at the east. Tahir stopped at the door to listen to the floor. He listened a long time.

"Slow," he said. "North wall." And then, looking at nobody: "The middle's bad."

They worked the north wall. Anu held the lamp and watched the beams. *Up* and nothing else. Midday, Meera said: "Anu. Take the lamp to the far end. Last cabinets. Stay on the wall."

Anu went.

North wall to the far end, where the floor was worst, where she'd seen the long cracks and the rust-water coming up through them. She stood at the edge and held the lamp high so they'd see her by it. Then she put it down by the wall, careful, turned it low, and stepped back into the dark.

The iron bar was where Meera had left it on the run before, pushed in behind the last cabinet. Anu set the end of it in the widest crack. She'd asked Meera how hard. *Not hard*, Meera said. *It's been waiting three hundred years.*

It wasn't hard.

The floor went with a sound like somebody sighing, and then a roar. A piece of floor big as a room tipped up and dropped into the dark under the hall and took the lamplight with it, and grey dust came up out of the hole and filled the west end all at once, rolling along the beams, stirring the mats.

Anu screamed. Once. As loud as she could. The dust ate it.

"*Out!*" Meera, huge, from the north wall. "Out! Jars first! Don't run, don't breathe it, out!"

She heard them going. Jai shouting her name, his voice cracking. Meera over him: *No. No. You know. Out.* Jars scraping. Tahir not saying anything at all. Then their lamp, going away along the north wall toward the door. Then gone.

She went the other way.

Through the dust with her eyes shut and her hood tight. South wall. East end. The fallen roof and the fig tree. She climbed out between its roots into the sun and kept going. Down into the forest on the far side, off the path, away from the post. When she'd gone far enough she stripped off the cloth and the hood and buried them under leaves. She stood in the shade in her undercloth, breathing air that didn't taste of anything, until her hands stopped.

They stopped. They always did.

---

She wasn't there for what happened at the post. Tahir told it later, the one time, years later, to someone else, and it came to her the way stories come. Meera touched the tongue and said the year, and the others said it after her, all but one. *One lost. The floor at the west end. It went from under the boy. He's beneath it.* The keeper looked at Meera a long time. Then he wrote a name and a line. He didn't ask to see the floor. Nobody ever did.

Jai cried by the fire that night, and Ravi sat with him. Tahir sat apart, looking up where the old stars sometimes moved. When Meera came and sat by him he said, "It was a good floor for it." And she said, "Yes." And that was the last either of them said about it, ever.

---

Nine days. Nine days of walking at night and lying up in the day. Two villages she went wide round, because a girl alone in a dead boy's undercloth gets looked at. One place where the road went through a post and there was no way round, and she waited a whole day in a ditch for a cart to hide behind. Her feet bled. She drank from tanks. She ate what she'd carried and then what she found.

The scribe met her on the steps of a little temple at the edge of the capital, in the grey before dawn. He was younger than she'd thought. Thin. Ink on his fingers. He kept his hands folded under a cloth bundle, the way the old smiths in the market fold theirs. It took her a while to see why.

He didn't say his name. She didn't ask.

In the bundle there was a sari. Plain cotton, no colour. And a folded paper, heavy, with the records hall's seal pressed in it. He read it out to her because she could only get pieces of it. A girl born free, in a village she'd never heard of. Parents dead in the fever two years back, so no one would come asking. Evaluation passed at fourteen. Apprentice's place at the copy-hall under the House of Readers. A name that was nearly Anu and nearly not.

"The twin's in the files," he said. "Anyone who looks finds the same thing."

"Did you read it?" she said. "The book."

He smiled then, first time. "I'm reading it. It's slow. The old language is like walking through deep water." He said a word she didn't know. Long. The fourteen shapes, said out loud. "That's how it sounds, I think. It means how a leaf makes itself out of light and air. They knew how. Every step. I think the rust is in some of the steps."

He unfolded his hands then, and she saw them shake. Not much. Fine and steady, like a cup of water on a table next to a busy road.

"My father was a smith," he said, when he saw her looking. "I worked his bellows from when I could stand. His metal from when I could lift it. Then I passed my evaluation and they taught me to read, and I came here." He held one hand up between them and watched it. "But the metal stays in you. Everybody gets the shakes in the end, you know. Men. Women. Smiths' sons sooner. Women later. Depends what you touched, and how little you were when you touched it."

"Can you still write?"

"For now. A few years. Then my letters will start to wander, and the Daftar has no use for a man whose letters wander." He shrugged. "So I've spent my nights on this. How plants are made. There's a caravan going north over the passes after the rains. I'll be on it. Up there they want this kind of knowing. They breed the grain up there, the kind that holds out against the rust, and the people who do it are half priests and half cooks. Plant alchemists, the traders call them. They'll take a man who can read the old leaf books, whatever his hands are doing." Almost a smile again. "By the time anyone looks close at the files, I'll be over the mountains."

Then he held out his shaking hand. She gave him back the slip of palm paper with the fourteen shapes on it, the last thing that joined the two of them. He folded it once and put it in his mouth and chewed and swallowed.

"We've never met," he said, and went down the steps into the dark of the lane.

---

She changed behind the temple wall. First time in a woman's clothes since she was fourteen. The sari was rough and she wound it wrong, twice, and had to start again, and nearly cried, and didn't. When it was on she stood in the first light and held her hands out in front of her and looked at them.

Steady. They were always steady. At the post that was the most dangerous thing about her. At the copy-hall it would be why they kept her.

She thought about the glass under the moss in the cold hall. Seventy-seven in one line, and dead in a week. She thought about Meera and Tahir and Jai crying by a fire for a boy who'd never been. She thought about Dev, whom she'd never met, and the baby that didn't breathe, and a woman who'd decided this time it would go another way. She thought about three bundles of yellow paper that had come out sewn against her skin, that no weigher had seen and no fire had burned, and that were worth more, to one thin, shaking man on his way north, than all the gold in the six jars.

Nobody had weighed them. That was what made them worth so much.

Then she walked into the capital to find the copy-hall, and the sun came up behind her, out of the east where the sea was, and nobody looked at her close at all.
